## 1. NorthCUA-Pro 397B: Open Computer-Use Agents Beat Trillion-Parameter Closed Models

### What Happened

On November 3 the NorthCUA team released a family of computer-use agents built on Qwen3.5 — Nano at 9B, Mini at 35B and Pro at 397B — together with weights, training pipelines, documentation and a Harness execution framework. The technical report claims Pro leads every panel of its headline comparison on desktop and web automation, and that even the 35B Mini beats one of the trillion-parameter closed systems on each benchmark. Training used an Experience Flywheel of more than 4 million GUI interaction steps, a harness for long-horizon sessions, and a staged curriculum of supervised fine-tuning followed by reinforcement learning. A NorthCUA-Ultrafast variant trades intermediate reasoning for direct action selection on the pre-RL Nano and reports 2.3x faster completion on matched successful WebArena tasks.

### Why It Matters

Computer use has been the clearest capability gap between closed frontier systems and open weights — it demands long-horizon state tracking, screen grounding and recovery from partial failures, none of which transfer well from text benchmarks. If a 397B open model genuinely matches trillion-parameter closed systems on OSWorld-Verified, the deployable surface for private desktop automation widens considerably: enterprises can run agents against internal applications without routing screen content through a vendor API. The caveat is that the comparison aggregates six protocols that do not share identical interfaces, so the ranking should be read as directional rather than like-for-like.

### Key Technical Points

- Three tiers from one recipe: Nano 9B, Mini 35B, Pro 397B, all initialized from Qwen3.5
- ScreenSpot-Pro 93.3, against 84.5 for Qwen3.8-Max and 67.7 for Kimi-K3
- OSWorld-Verified 88.6; WebArena-Verified 73.9; OSWorld 2.0 partial credit 66.8
- Experience Flywheel collected over 4 million GUI interaction steps across apps and browsers
- Released artifacts include weights, training code, docs and the Harness execution framework
- NorthCUA-Ultrafast skips chain-of-thought on the pre-RL Nano for 2.3x faster action selection

## 2. CrowdStrike Ships an AI Kill Switch and Buys Fiddler and Dreadnode for About $495 Million

### What Happened

At Fal.Con Europe on November 3, CrowdStrike announced an AI Kill Switch inside Falcon Guardian: an authorized administrator can stop a misbehaving agent, block it from relaunching on targeted hosts and cut its enterprise network access, with every action written to an audit trail. The company also signed definitive agreements to acquire Fiddler AI for agent observability and Dreadnode for automated AI security testing. The Wall Street Journal reported roughly $400 million for Fiddler and about $95 million for Dreadnode; CrowdStrike confirmed the structure is mostly cash with some stock subject to vesting. One limitation is disclosed plainly: the stop currently takes effect at the gateway first, with enforcement on the endpoint described as coming soon.

### Why It Matters

Agent governance has spent a year producing frameworks; this is the first large security vendor shipping a stop button as product, paired with the two capabilities that make a stop button trustworthy — knowing what the agent did, and having tested it before it shipped. The gateway-first, endpoint-later sequencing is the interesting engineering detail: it implies the vendor believes network egress is both the highest-leverage control point and the easiest to enforce without kernel-level agents. The gap it leaves open is precisely the case enterprises fear most, an agent acting entirely within its own host.

### Key Technical Points

- Kill Switch stops a running agent, blocks relaunch on targeted hosts, cuts enterprise network access
- Every action logged in an audit trail
- Enforcement order: gateway first, endpoint sensor later; full endpoint stop not yet available
- Fiddler acquisition brings agent-level observability and failure attribution
- Dreadnode brings continuous pre-deployment testing of models, applications and agents
- Reported consideration about $400M for Fiddler and about $95M for Dreadnode, mostly cash plus vesting stock

## 3. Drax and Brookfield Plan a $27 Billion Gigawatt Data Centre at a British Power Station

### What Happened

Drax Group shares rose more than 8% on November 3 after the FTSE 250 power producer announced a partnership with Brookfield and its UK AI infrastructure platform Radiant to develop a data centre at Drax Power Station in North Yorkshire. Capacity is targeted above 1 GW in three phases: up to 100 MW from the first quarter of 2028, roughly 500 MW more between 2028 and 2031, and further expansion after that. Brookfield, funding through a joint venture with Qatar's Qai, estimates about £2.5 billion for phase one and more than £20 billion (about $26.8 billion) if built out fully. Drax contributes land, cooling and an existing 4 GW grid connection alongside 2.6 GW of active generation.

### Why It Matters

The scarce input for AI capacity has shifted from accelerators to interconnect queues, and this deal is the clearest illustration yet: the project's advantage is not a new substation but a grid connection that already exists. It also shows how the ownership structure is changing — a utility contributes the physical site, an infrastructure fund contributes the balance sheet through a sovereign co-investment vehicle, and an AI platform operator contributes the tenant-facing layer. Two things remain unproven: formal planning has not been filed, regulatory and network approvals are still pending, and no tenant has been announced.

### Key Technical Points

- Potential investment above £20B (about $26.8B) including semiconductors, per CNBC
- Phase 1: up to 100 MW, commercial operation targeted Q1 2028, about £2.5B
- Phase 2: additional 500 MW between 2028 and 2031; further phases targeted in the 2030s
- Site assets: 1,000 acres, 4 GW grid access, 2.6 GW active generation, cooling and nearby fibre
- Capital from the Brookfield-Qai AI infrastructure joint venture via Radiant
- Direct power supply to the site targeted for the early 2030s, subject to regulatory approval

## 4. Fractile Raises About $774 Million at a $6.5 Billion Valuation Before Shipping Any Chip

### What Happened

London-based Fractile has allotted roughly £577 million, about $774 million, of Series C shares since September according to Companies House filings first reported by Tech Funding News, putting the inference-chip startup near a reported $6.5 billion valuation, up from about $1 billion in May. The round has not been formally announced. Fractile builds inference silicon that places memory next to the compute transistors and targets 1,200 tokens per second on workloads it says currently run near 40. Bloomberg reported an initial agreement to sell Anthropic about $250 million of chips, with deployment expected in 2027. Reported investors include Lightspeed, Redpoint, Thrive Capital and the UK Sovereign AI Fund.

### Why It Matters

This is the cleanest datapoint yet that AI capital is being priced on a claimed architectural advantage rather than on shipped product: a company with no commercial silicon raised more in one round than most chip startups raise in a decade, and secured a named anchor customer at roughly 30x its valuation six months earlier. The bet is specific — memory bandwidth, not FLOPs, is the binding constraint on inference, so keeping weights physically adjacent to the multiply units attacks the actual bottleneck. The risk is equally specific: from simulation to a fab tape-out to volume deployment in 2027 is where most novel memory architectures historically stall.

### Key Technical Points

- About £577M (~$774M) Series C allotted across September and October per Companies House filings
- Reported valuation near $6.5B, versus about $1B in May 2026; total raised about $941M
- Approach: in-memory compute, keeping weights adjacent to processing transistors
- Target 1,200 tokens/s against roughly 40 tokens/s on some current long workloads
- Reported initial Anthropic order worth about $250M, deployment targeted 2027
- No official announcement as of November 3; reported leads Lightspeed and Redpoint

## 5. Flower Labs Ships Endeavor 1.1 with Image Input and EU-Located Inference

### What Happened

Flower Labs released Endeavor 1.1 on November 3, its third model of the year after the 7B Lizzy and Endeavor 1.0. The update adds native image input and an option to run inference on GPUs physically located in the EU, backed by service-level agreements, alongside the existing global cloud offering. Published scores: GPQA 94.4 (up from 92.0), HumanEval 99.9, IFEval 94.5, AIME 2026 99.9, and a new MMMU-Pro score of 81.1. Flower frames the product around deployment control rather than raw capability — customers keep access to thinking traces and the right to train their own models on generated outputs. Access is by request rather than open weights.

### Why It Matters

The interesting part is not the benchmark row but the positioning: a frontier-tier model sold on data residency and auditability, with a UK-sovereign lineage and an explicit comparison against Azure's UK-only per-token offering. That is a bet that European enterprises will pay a capability premium for a model whose inference never leaves the region and whose reasoning can be inspected. It also matters that Flower is not open-sourcing: the offer is control without custody, which keeps weights closed while conceding the deployment layer. Whether that middle position survives depends on whether private-cloud customers treat it as meaningfully different from a self-hosted open model.

### Key Technical Points

- GPQA 94.4 from 92.0 — roughly 30% fewer errors; HumanEval 98.2 to 99.9; IFEval 94.1 to 94.5
- AIME 2026 99.9, tied with GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Fable 5
- New modality: native image input, MMMU-Pro 81.1
- EU-located GPU inference with SLAs, in addition to the global offering
- Thinking traces are exposed to customers; outputs are licensed for training their own models
- Access by request; weights are not released, unlike the open-weight competitors it benchmarks against

# Important Paper of the Day

## Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists

Du, Zhao, Wang, Lu, Zhang and Tao introduce OmniQ, an expert-audited virtual research environment that converts published quantum papers into executable reproduction tasks, then evaluate five LLM-powered agentic systems across six third-party agent harnesses on 59 papers spanning classical simulation, quantum algorithms and quantum-property characterization. The headline finding is negative: current AI scientists frequently fail to reproduce published results, exposing a gap between what papers communicate and what autonomous agents need in order to act on them. The constructive half is that targeted human expert intervention converts those failures into successful reproductions, and the missing information can be identified and written back into a form agents can consume.

Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02833

Rather than building another GUI agent model, it standardises the action layer underneath: agents drive real desktop applications through a command interface instead of embedding a bespoke automation stack. # Trend Observation

Three stories today describe the same structural shift from three directions. NorthCUA shows that the binding constraint on computer-use agents was never raw parameter count but interface-specialised training data and a stable harness — a 397B open model with 4 million collected GUI steps can displace trillion-parameter closed systems, which relocates the moat from weights to the harness and the data flywheel. CrowdStrike shows where the governance debate has actually landed: not in statutes, but in a product SKU with a stop button, an audit trail and two acquisitions that supply the evidence layer. And Fractile plus Drax/Brookfield show capital stretching in both directions — $774M into a chip that has not taped out, $27B into a building whose advantage is a grid connection that already exists. The common thread is that the scarce inputs have moved off the model: to harnesses and GUI trajectories, to enforcement and audit, and to silicon and interconnection. AMD's quarter makes the same point in numbers — data centre revenue of $8.5B, up 97% year over year, is still roughly a tenth of NVIDIA's $89B, which is why inference-chip challengers with a claimed architectural edge can raise at a $6.5B valuation before shipping anything.
