# AI Daily Digest - 2026-11-08

## Executive Summary

The measurement layer moved this week and the story about open weights moved with it. Artificial Analysis overhauled its suite, kept many items private, and the best open-weight model immediately fell from within three points of Claude Opus 5.5 to twelve points behind, with even Claude Haiku 5.5 outscoring every downloadable model. That is either a correction of a leaked benchmark or an admission that public evals were never measuring what buyers thought. In parallel, Google DeepMind published cryptanalysis in which human-guided agents found two weaknesses in legacy 1024-bit RSA within weeks, and Vals showed a free DeepSeek model completing 73% of the objectives OpenAI's restricted cyber agent did at roughly a tenth of the price. Underneath, Microsoft is winding down the AI capacity it rents from Oracle, and Alphabet's freshly disclosed Qualcomm stake is a reminder that accelerator exposure is being diversified at the equity level, not just the procurement level.

## 1. Artificial Analysis Goes Private, and the Open-Weight Gap Triples

### What Happened

Artificial Analysis, the independent benchmarking firm most of the industry quotes, has overhauled its evaluation system so that a substantial share of its tests are no longer public. The immediate consequence reported this week: the best model with publicly released weights now sits at about 32 on the revamped index against 58 for Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5, a gap of roughly twelve points where the previous public suite showed about three. Even Claude Haiku 5.5, a deliberately smaller tier, outscored every model whose weights can be downloaded. Usage data complicates the picture in the other direction: Chinese models still account for about 65% of tokens routed through OpenRouter but only about 29% of spending.

### Why It Matters

The "cheap Chinese open-weight model is nearly as good" thesis has been load-bearing for pricing, for procurement and for Anthropic's own IPO narrative, and it was built almost entirely on public leaderboards. If the gap was largely an artifact of models being tuned against known questions, then two things change at once: the frontier labs' price premium gets a defensible technical justification, and every vendor-reported benchmark in this digest becomes less trustworthy. The honest reading is not that open models got worse overnight, but that the industry has been quoting a number whose denominator nobody audited. Vendors now have an incentive to publish their own private-suite results, which reintroduces the same selection problem one layer up.

### Key Technical Points

- Revamped index places Claude Opus 5.5 at about 58 with the best open-weight model near 32, versus a roughly three-point gap on the previous public suite
- Claude Haiku 5.5, the smaller Anthropic tier, outscores every publicly downloadable model on the private suite
- Chinese models account for roughly 65% of OpenRouter tokens but only about 29% of spend
- Snowflake CEO Sridhar Ramaswamy: "The day benchmarks went private, the open-model gap went from 3 points to 12"
- Mechanism: public fixed question sets allow targeted tuning, the practice Microsoft's Satya Nadella and others call benchmark hacking
- Caveat: private suites cannot be independently replicated, so the correction is itself unaudited

## 2. Human-Guided DeepMind Agents Break Ground Under Legacy 1024-Bit RSA

### What Happened

On November 6 Google DeepMind said AI agents working under human supervision, alongside academic cryptanalysts, found two distinct weaknesses in legacy 1024-bit RSA within a span of weeks. The work was led by Dj Dvijotham and Abhradeep Thakurta with Nadia Heninger of UC San Diego and Inria and Emmanuel Thomé of Inria; Pushmeet Kohli announced the result. The first finding is a signature forgery method that, when the attacker controls only a small part of the signed data such as a 32-byte digest, is in theory about 1,600 times faster than the previous best method for 1024-bit keys. A sandbox test reproduced a known RSA-1024 forgery using 3.5 times less CPU time than earlier academic work. The second is a batch factoring method whose asymptotic complexity beats earlier batch techniques, cutting the average cost of factoring each additional 1024-bit key by roughly 30 times.

### Why It Matters

DeepMind's own framing is the important part: the results do not threaten 2048-bit keys, which cover banking, messaging and software updates, but they undermine the habit of treating computational hardness as a stable assumption with a long shelf life. If a supervised agent plus two domain experts can move a decades-old cost estimate by three orders of magnitude in weeks, then the review cadence for cryptographic standards, which is measured in years, is mismatched to the tool that is now available. It also previews a governance problem: the same capability is equally useful for auditing an institution's legacy fleet and for finding the systems that never migrated.

### Key Technical Points

- Signature forgery: about 1,600x faster than the prior best in theory for 1024-bit keys when the attacker controls a small portion of signed data such as a 32-byte digest
- Sandbox reproduction of a known RSA-1024 forgery with 3.5x less CPU time than earlier academic work
- Batch factoring of same-length moduli with better asymptotic complexity, cutting per-additional-key cost by about 30x
- Scope: 1024-bit RSA only; 2048-bit and larger keys are not affected by these specific results
- Team: DeepMind's Dj Dvijotham and Abhradeep Thakurta with Nadia Heninger (UC San Diego, Inria) and Emmanuel Thomé (Inria)
- DeepMind's stated conclusion: historical assumptions about computational difficulty can no longer be relied on and standards need continuous stress testing

## 3. A Free DeepSeek Model Reaches 73% of OpenAI's Restricted Cyber Agent

### What Happened

Vals, a benchmarking firm, ran seven models as autonomous agents against simulated corporate networks and reported the results via WIRED. OpenAI's GPT-6 Sol, available only through the restricted Daybreak cyber program, completed about 81% of objectives; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, an open-weight model anyone can download, completed about 73%. On one range DeepSeek averaged 16.3 objectives against Daybreak's 15.7 and matched it outright on the first network, for under $3 a run versus roughly $22. Daybreak pulled ahead on a harder range, finishing 27 of 31 objectives to DeepSeek's 21. Cost per full attack was $2 to $3 for DeepSeek against $17 to $46 for GPT-6 Sol. Z.ai's GLM-5.3 Flash came last of seven, with Vals calling it "the clearest dead end" after roughly 36 hours largely spent polling a background job that never returned.

### Why It Matters

The interesting finding is methodological. DeepSeek completed nothing in its first 25 million tokens and did most of its work after 100 million, which means short compute-capped evaluations systematically understate cheap models and flatter expensive ones. That is the same class of error as the public-benchmark problem above, arriving from the opposite direction: one measurement artifact inflated open-weight capability, another deflated it. Vals' conclusion that real capability is a function of budget rather than of per-token quality reframes the policy question, because the thing that gates access is no longer weights but the willingness to spend tokens.

### Key Technical Points

- Test: seven models run as autonomous agents against simulated corporate networks
- GPT-6 Sol via Daybreak: about 81% of objectives; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash: about 73%
- Full attack cost: $2 to $3 for DeepSeek against $17 to $46 for GPT-6 Sol
- DeepSeek produced nothing in the first 25M tokens; most progress came after 100M, so compute-capped short runs understate it
- Head-to-head on one range: DeepSeek 16.3 average objectives against Daybreak's 15.7
- Divergence elsewhere: NIST called full GLM-5.3 the most capable open-weight cyber model to date, roughly four months behind leading US systems; the Flash variant scored last here
- Caveat: simulated networks are not live systems

## 4. Microsoft Walks Away From Oracle's AI Capacity, and Old GPUs Get Marked Down

### What Happened

Oracle shares fell to $109.21 on November 6, a new 52-week low, after people familiar with the matter said Microsoft is winding down its deal to rent AI computing capacity from Oracle. Oracle pushed back with data: earlier-generation GPUs coming up for renewal were renewed or rented to new customers at a 20% premium, the majority of that fleet is four years or older, and fleet utilization was 97.9% that quarter. Oracle's cloud business brought in $18 billion in the year ended May 2026, with most of the growth coming from AI contracts far larger than the Microsoft arrangement.

### Why It Matters

Two readings coexist and the market is pricing the pessimistic one. Either Oracle is right that depreciated accelerators still clear a premium, in which case GPU useful life is longer than the bears assume and the whole depreciation curve underpinning neocloud economics is too conservative; or Microsoft, holding the pricing power in this relationship, decided its own newer capacity and custom silicon beat renting someone else's four-year-old fleet, in which case the residual value of installed accelerators is not an asset but a liability with a renewal date. Both readings matter for the same reason: this is the first large contract where a hyperscaler chose between its own silicon and a partner's ageing GPUs in public.

### Key Technical Points

- Oracle closed at $109.21 on November 6, a 52-week low, on reports Microsoft is winding down rented AI capacity
- Oracle: renewed GPUs cleared at a 20% premium, majority four years or older, utilization 97.9% for the quarter
- Oracle cloud revenue: $18 billion for the year ended May 2026, growth driven by AI contracts larger than this one
- Microsoft's leverage comes from newer in-house silicon plus a soft AI capex environment after the recent selloff
- The unresolved question is residual value and useful life assumptions across the neocloud sector

## 5. Alphabet Discloses a $548M Qualcomm Stake Sourced From the Modular Deal

### What Happened

Alphabet disclosed a Qualcomm stake worth nearly $548 million, obtained when Google Ventures received shares as part of Qualcomm's acquisition of Modular. Qualcomm reaffirmed a $5 billion fiscal 2027 data-center revenue target on its November 4 earnings call, and is steering toward more than $15 billion in annual data-center revenue by fiscal 2029 alongside $40 billion in non-handset revenue. Google is simultaneously expanding its own TPU programme and has a custom-silicon partnership with Marvell.

### Why It Matters

This is an equity-level hedge rather than a procurement decision, and it is the cleaner signal of how hyperscalers are managing accelerator risk: Google is buying its own TPUs, partnering with Marvell on custom silicon, and now holding paper exposure to the company trying to sell a fourth compatible stack. Modular's importance is that its software is designed to target multiple architectures rather than locking to CUDA, so owning a slice of that company is a bet on portability itself becoming valuable. For Qualcomm the stake is third-party validation at the exact moment it needs the data-center story to replace a softening handset business.

### Key Technical Points

- Alphabet stake: nearly $548 million in Qualcomm shares, received by Google Ventures via Qualcomm's Modular acquisition
- Qualcomm targets: $5 billion fiscal 2027 data-center revenue reaffirmed, more than $15 billion annually by fiscal 2029, $40 billion non-handset
- Modular software targets multiple architectures rather than locking to CUDA, positioning portability as the product
- Google runs TPUs in parallel and has a custom-silicon partnership with Marvell, so the stake is additive rather than a switch
- Context: Qualcomm's smartphone business is under pressure while the Dragonfly accelerator family and custom silicon build out

# Important Paper of the Day

## A Machine-Checked Proof of the Aaronson–Ambainis Conjecture

Physical Superintelligence (PSI) announced on November 6 that an internal research system running instances of a non-public Anthropic model produced a proof of the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture, posed in 2008 and central to when quantum computers can achieve exponential speedups. The system reached a complete proof on the third day of a run on PSI's own infrastructure; Lean accepted it, and the system then improved the result twice before Lean accepted the final version. The manuscript and the Lean 4 formalization are public.

The substance, as PSI states it, is that bounded polynomials of low degree have influential variables, which implies quantum computers are at most polynomially faster than classical ones unless the inputs carry special structure such as the exponential structure in Shor's algorithm. Guy Blanc, Jordan Docter, Carmen Strassle and Li-Yang Tan, who settled a special case in August 2026, have read the proof and are preparing a shorter expert exposition; they called it a huge result that resolves a decades-old conjecture.

The honest caveats matter as much as the result. It has not been peer reviewed or accepted by a journal; Lean verifies the encoded argument, but somebody still has to confirm the encoded statement is the conjecture as Aaronson and Ambainis posed it, and the parts of the paper outside Lean still need expert reading. PSI also did not name the Anthropic model. The verifiable claim today is narrower than the headline: a machine-checked formal artifact exists, and the human review is in progress.

# Important GitHub Project

## elder-plinius/T3MP3ST

An autonomous red-teaming platform and multi-agent offensive-security meta-harness, written in TypeScript under AGPL-3.0, at roughly 6,973 stars and 1,447 forks with about 242 stars added in a single day. It orchestrates multiple specialised attacker agents against a target rather than running one prompt loop, which is the same architecture the industry has spent the week discovering on the defensive side: capability scales with budget and with agent count more than with any single model's quality. Note the asymmetry before adopting it — the repository makes offensive tooling reproducible for anyone, and the studies above show simulated attack capability transferring poorly to live systems in both directions.

# Trend Observation

Three of today's five stories are the same story told from different angles: the industry cannot currently measure what it claims to measure. A benchmark that goes private cuts the open-weight gap threefold; a test that caps compute understates a cheap model by a third; a cryptanalytic result that took weeks invalidates cost assumptions that were supposed to hold for decades. Each one is a case where the artifact being trusted was a proxy, and the proxy failed in a direction that favoured whoever paid for it. The practical response is not cynicism about benchmarks but a change in what gets requested: budget-normalised evaluations, released compute traces alongside scores, and results that a third party can replay rather than read.

The infrastructure story rhymes. Oracle's argument that four-year-old accelerators still clear a 20% premium is a claim about useful life, which is exactly the kind of assumption that no one has independently audited either. Alphabet buying a slice of Qualcomm through the Modular deal is the more revealing move: rather than betting on one accelerator roadmap, it is holding equity in portability itself. When the measurement layer and the depreciation schedule are both in question, capital starts pricing optionality instead of a specific forecast.
