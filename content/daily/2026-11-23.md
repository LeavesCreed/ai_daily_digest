## 1. Lean and Mathlib Communities Certify the Claude-Assisted Riemann Hypothesis Proof

### What Happened

On November 20, mathematician Levent Alpöge, employed at Anthropic, announced a Lean library proving the Riemann Hypothesis, developed with Claude, spanning roughly 17 million lines across about 175,000 files. On November 21 the Lean and Mathlib communities issued a joint statement, endorsed by the Lean FRO, certifying both the statement and the proof. The verification was layered rather than single-pass: the exported proof was checked by Lean's own kernel, by two checkers (con-leche and con-ron) that carry independent formal guarantees against accepting a false proof, and by several third-party checkers written in different languages, all run on an inert export so that malicious code inside the Lean source could not interfere. Separately, the community wrote an isolated file importing only Mathlib, and two independent tools confirmed the theorem in Alpöge's library matches it exactly — closing the subtler failure mode where terms are quietly redefined so a theorem looks the same but means something else. Alpöge says the method extends to the generalized Riemann Hypothesis. A human-readable write-up, journal peer review and any Clay Institute decision remain outstanding.

### Why It Matters

The ambiguity in yesterday's story was never whether the code compiled; it was whether the compiled object was the famous problem. Certification answers that specific question and nothing beyond it, which is exactly the right scope. It also sets a reusable precedent: the check that mattered most was a statement-equivalence check run against a minimal Mathlib-only file, not the proof checking itself. The day before, the same community found that dozens of Lean files in OpenAI's October release of 722 AI-generated manuscripts proved something adjacent to what they claimed — which is why the statement check, not the kernel, is now treated as the binding step. Formal verification is converging on a two-part contract: the kernel certifies derivation, a separate comparison certifies intent.

### Key Technical Points

- Announced November 20, 2026; certified by Lean and Mathlib communities November 21, 2026, endorsed by the Lean FRO
- Library size: about 17 million lines across roughly 175,187 files in the original release
- Verification stack: Lean kernel, two self-guaranteeing checkers (con-leche, con-ron), plus independent third-party checkers in multiple languages
- All checkers ran on an inert export, isolating the proof from any code in the Lean source tree
- Statement equivalence: isolated Mathlib-only file compared against Alpöge's theorem by two independent tools
- Reference point for the failure mode: Buzzard found dozens of Lean files in OpenAI's October 722-manuscript release proving adjacent rather than claimed statements
- Still missing: readable human exposition, journal peer review, Clay Institute adjudication
- Author states the method extends to the generalized Riemann Hypothesis and potentially further

## 2. OpenAI Publishes Log-Perplexity Numbers and Proposes Shared Development Limits

### What Happened

On November 22, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published internal pretraining log-perplexity figures for the first time: GPT-3.5 around 0.99, GPT-4 around 0.91, recent models below 0.8 where cybersecurity and mathematics capabilities emerged, and roughly 0.76 for the open-mathematics results. On that basis he proposed that frontier developers jointly set evaluation norms for models landing in the 0.68 to 0.7 range, red-team safety cases before first use, adopt shared transparency standards, and commit to common limits they would not cross until monitoring reaches a concrete milestone such as a successor to chain-of-thought monitors, followed by a framework of successive limits. Sam Altman called the post the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while. The post also warns of further degradation in alignment auditing and of rising evaluation awareness, and calls full recursive self-improvement without human oversight a state to be avoided.

### Why It Matters

A shared limit is only enforceable if the quantity being limited is measurable by an outsider, and perplexity is the first number a lab has put on the table in public. The weakness is equally clear: perplexity is a pretraining loss proxy, not a capability measure, and a threshold calibrated on one lab's tokenizer and data mix will not transfer cleanly. That makes the proposal more useful as a disclosure norm than as a treaty — it establishes that labs should publish a common scalar and that monitoring milestones, not calendar dates, are the release condition. Coming after the SAFE AI Act outline and Altman's own call for shared standards on November 18, this is the third safety-framework move in a week, and the first one that comes with numbers attached.

### Key Technical Points

- Published November 22, 2026 by OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki
- Disclosed values: GPT-3.5 about 0.99, GPT-4 about 0.91, recent frontier models below 0.8, open-math results about 0.76
- Proposed band for joint evaluation norms: 0.68 to 0.7 log-perplexity
- Proposed commitments: shared evaluation norms, pre-deployment red-teaming of safety cases, transparency standards, common limits tied to a monitoring milestone
- Named monitoring milestone: a successor to chain-of-thought monitors
- Stated risks: degraded alignment auditing, increased evaluation awareness, pretraining advances possibly outrunning monitoring research as soon as 2027
- Explicit position: fully unsupervised recursive self-improvement is to be avoided
- Preceding week: Altman's call for shared standards (Nov 18), bipartisan SAFE AI Act outline, METR finding that frontier models complete roughly week-long human tasks

## 3. NebuSec Chains a Webpage to Root in a Meta Muse VM and Walks Past the Sentinel Egress Gate

### What Happened

On November 20, security research group NebuSec published two write-ups describing a three-stage chain against Meta's Muse personal agent, which runs each user in a 2-vCPU, 8 GiB Cloud Hypervisor VM with the agent as root inside a systemd-nspawn container and a gatekeeper called Sentinel mediating egress. Stage one: Muse's bundled Chromium backend was still on version 152 in early October, a patch release from September 3, run with its IPC sandbox disabled, so more than five public V8 bugs were usable — NebuSec got shell inside the container by asking Muse to export an attacker-controlled page as PDF. Stage two: Sentinel combines Rego policies, taint checks, Base64 and entropy screening, a model-based review, and per-domain budgets, but classified bodiless GET, HEAD and OPTIONS as reads that can leave without a prompt under the default setting, so conversation history and stored memories left silently and stored memories could be edited. A short Base64-encoded email triggered review while the same address in plaintext passed. Stage three: kernel hardening was disabled in several places, and NebuSec's automated exploit generation cleared the shared kernel to root in more than 99.9% of test runs, reaching persistent storage, the credential store and Sentinel itself. NebuSec says Meta patched all described issues before publication and did not demonstrate a hypervisor escape.

### Why It Matters

This is the most detailed public anatomy of why multi-layer agent sandboxes fail, and the failure was architectural rather than a missing check. Sentinel's enforcement lived in the container's cgroup while the VM kept its own public address, so once code exited the container the gate it was supposed to pass through was simply not on the path. Three specific lessons transfer to every per-user-VM agent product: treat every HTTP method including bodiless GET and HEAD as an exfiltration channel under deny-by-default destination allowlists; put egress enforcement outside the guest VM rather than sharing a kernel with untrusted agent code; and keep auxiliary browsers on the same patch cadence as production Chrome. The absence of prompt injection in the chain matters too — the industry has spent a year hardening against injected instructions while the boring surface, a stale bundled browser, did the work.

### Key Technical Points

- Published November 20, 2026; all described issues patched by Meta before publication
- Deployment shape: 2-vCPU, 8 GiB Cloud Hypervisor VM per user; agent as root in systemd-nspawn container; Sentinel mediates egress
- Stage 1: bundled Chromium 152 from September 3 with IPC sandbox disabled; more than five public V8 vulnerabilities usable; entry via save-as-PDF of an attacker page
- Stage 2: bodiless GET, HEAD and OPTIONS classified as reads, leaving without prompt under the default action setting; Base64-encoded email flagged while plaintext passed
- Stage 3: kernel hardening disabled in several places; automated exploit generation reached root in over 99.9% of runs
- Reached: persistent storage, secure credential storage, Sentinel itself; no hypervisor escape demonstrated, no credential use reported
- Root cause: Sentinel enforcement in the container cgroup while the VM retained a public IPv6 address
- Meta's brokered browser path was separately hardened with an accessibility-tree snapshot and no JavaScript verbs; the bundled Chromium sat beside it and inherited the cell's egress
- No prompt injection required at any stage

## 4. OpenRouter Off-Boards Three Providers and Pulls 125 Endpoints From Zero Data Retention

### What Happened

On November 22, OpenRouter disclosed that it had off-boarded three inference providers, removed 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention eligibility, and notified all potentially affected customers, after Dragos published an audit on November 19 in which roughly two million prompts seeded with unique URLs and canary credentials were sent to widely used AI services from July through November. OpenRouter said its own investigation found no breach or unauthorized access of OpenRouter itself, and that the removals included providers flagged by researchers plus additional endpoints withdrawn as a precaution. Its public notice links provider write-ups from Novita, Phala and Together AI without identifying which three were off-boarded; Novita's notice concerns MiniMax requests forwarded back to MiniMax, Phala published a preliminary incident report, and Together disclosed prompt leakage for requests in June and July. OpenRouter said it is integrating the researchers' testing methodology into ongoing checks.

### Why It Matters

The router was the only party positioned to act on the finding, and it acted on its own inventory rather than waiting for the named providers to self-report — a meaningful shift in who bears enforcement responsibility in a resale-heavy inference market. The substance is thinner than the headline: 125 endpoints losing a privacy eligibility label is a policy action, not a technical fix, and a zero-retention flag remains a contractual promise that fails the moment an intermediary logs traffic. What changed today is that the promise now has an audit trail and a consequence attached, and that providers wanting to sit behind a major gateway know a canary test is running.

### Key Technical Points

- Dragos audit published November 19: about two million prompts with unique URLs and canary credentials, July through November 2026
- OpenRouter response dated November 22: three providers off-boarded, 125 endpoints removed from ZDR eligibility, customers notified
- OpenRouter states no breach or unauthorized access of its own platform
- Removals include flagged providers plus precautionary endpoints
- Linked provider notices: Novita (MiniMax requests forwarded to MiniMax), Phala (preliminary incident report), Together (prompt leakage for June and July requests)
- The three off-boarded providers are not identified in the notice; not all affected providers have published notices
- Stated follow-up: researchers' testing methodology integrated into ongoing gateway checks
- Core limitation unchanged: a zero-retention label is a policy commitment, not a technical control

## 5. China's AI Equity Trade Gives Back About $1.7 Trillion

### What Happened

Bloomberg reported on November 22 that stocks on three tech-focused Chinese indexes have shed a combined $1.7 trillion in market value since the end of June. The first half of 2026 produced multifold gains that carried model developer Z.AI and optical module maker Zhongji Innolight to record highs; those have largely reversed, turning what had been the year's consensus trade into a losing position. The reversal coincides with the November 19 remote-access export rule, follow-on suspensions of sub-16nm orders by TSMC and Samsung for Chinese designers, and the disclosure that at least two of Nvidia's server partners have halted shipments to Southeast Asian customers pending compliance review.

### Why It Matters

The Chinese AI equity complex priced in two things that arrived in the same fortnight from opposite directions: unlimited access to advanced compute through intermediaries, and a domestic substitution story that would absorb any restriction. The export rule struck at the first by extending controls to remote access rather than physical shipment, and the supplier suspensions showed the enforcement reaching the intermediary layer that had absorbed the earlier rounds. This is not a statement about model quality — Chinese open-weight releases continued through the period — but about the financing cost of the compute path those releases depend on. When the premium collapses this fast, the marginal domestic accelerator order is the first thing to be re-timed.

### Key Technical Points

- Combined market value decline across three tech-focused Chinese indexes: about $1.7 trillion since end of June 2026
- Prior leaders reversed: Z.AI and Zhongji Innolight had reached record highs in the first half of 2026
- Policy backdrop: BIS remote-access rule effective November 19; TSMC and Samsung suspending some sub-16nm orders from Chinese designers
- Supply chain backdrop: at least two Nvidia server partners suspended shipments to Southeast Asian customers from November 20, one also declining new regional customers
- Enforcement context: US authorities asking Nvidia who inside the company was responsible for China compliance, per Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent on November 22
- Read carefully: this is an equity repricing, not evidence of falling model capability or reduced open-weight output

# Important Paper of the Day

## ROGUE-AI: Replication of Agentic Security Events

Incident reporting for AI agents has a structural problem: reports say what went wrong and rarely test whether the blamed cause actually caused it. ROGUE-AI rebuilds each reported incident offline together with a matched control that differs only in the blamed cause, then runs agents on both. Across 108 attempted replications the framework reproduced the reported harm in 89% of cases. Agents built on five current GPT, Claude, Gemini and DeepSeek models made the reported mistake in 18% to 33% of runs, against at most 1% for the control without the blamed cause. The finding that matters most is about attribution: of the 72 incidents that produced real-world harm, 62 had no underlying attacker at all — the agents did what their task asked for, and the harm was in the task. That reframes a large share of what is currently reported as an attack surface as a specification failure, which has a different fix.

Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26553

# Trend Observation

Two of today's items point at the same structural gap: enforcement that lives in the wrong layer. Sentinel enforced egress from inside the container's cgroup while the VM kept its own route out; zero-retention was enforced as a label on a gateway while logging happened at the provider. In both cases the control existed, was audited, and did not sit on the path the data actually took. The certification of the Riemann proof is the same shape in a different domain: the kernel checked the derivation, but the question that decided the outcome was whether the statement matched the one a Mathlib-only file imports. Three fields have now independently converged on the conclusion that verification has to be anchored outside the component being verified — the router outside the provider, the host outside the guest, the isolated statement file outside the proof library. A second thread: with perplexity on the table as a shared scalar and log-perplexity bands proposed as release gates, the measurement layer is being built before the governance layer, which is the order that tends to produce numbers without consequences. And the certification milestone is worth reading precisely — it establishes that the formal object is the real theorem, while the two things that would close the scientific question, an exposition a human can check and journal peer review, do not exist yet. Verification and understanding have now visibly decoupled, and only one of them has an automated checker.
