## 1. Moonshot Ships Kimi K3.1 Pro: 2.8T Parameters, Weights Promised by November 4

### What Happened

Moonshot AI released Kimi K3.1 Pro on October 23, 2026, the first entry in a new K3.1 series, alongside a faster companion called K3.1 Pro Highspeed. The model carries roughly 2.8 trillion total parameters with 104 billion active per token through a mixture-of-experts architecture, a 1,048,576-token context window, and text, image and video input. Moonshot describes it as a post-training pass over the Kimi K3 base rather than a new base model, with substantially more compute in that phase and reworked reinforcement learning, long-context inference and agent sandboxes. On Moonshot's own published numbers it scores 65.7% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 against 66.4% for Claude Opus 5.5. API pricing is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. Full open weights are promised by November 4, with no license named yet. The company said it deliberately stripped cyber-related data from both the mid-training corpus and the RL environment mix for the open release, while keeping general coding capability.

### Why It Matters

The strategic content here is the two-week gap between API access and weight publication. Moonshot has met that kind of promise before - K3 shipped July 16 and the weights landed on Hugging Face July 27 - so the November 4 date is a real commitment, not a marketing placeholder. Artificial Analysis projects that once the files exist, K3.1 Pro becomes the highest-scoring open-weight model on its index and the gap between the best open and best proprietary model narrows from about 12 points to about 3. That is the difference between open weights being a cost-optimization option and open weights being a credible default for teams that cannot accept vendor lock-in. The explicit decision to remove cyber-training data while keeping coding also sets a precedent: capability-scoped open release as a repeatable practice rather than a one-off concession.

### Key Technical Points

- 2.8T total parameters, 104B active per token, MoE; 1,048,576-token context; text, image and video input
- Post-training of K3 with far more compute in that phase; changes to the RL system, long-context inference and agent sandboxes
- Terminal-Bench 4.0: 65.7% self-reported versus 66.4% for Claude Opus 5.5; no independent evaluation yet
- Pricing: $2 per million input tokens, $10 per million output tokens; model IDs kimi-k3.1-pro and kimi-k3.1-pro-highspeed
- Cyber-related data removed from mid-training corpus and RL environment mix for the open release; general coding retained
- Weights promised by November 4, 2026; license not specified at announcement
- Artificial Analysis projection: open-versus-proprietary gap on its index narrows from roughly 12 points to roughly 3

## 2. Microsoft's MAI-DxO Beats Generalist Physicians by More Than Twofold in Science

### What Happened

A peer-reviewed study published October 22, 2026 in Science reports that Microsoft's MAI Diagnostic Orchestrator (MAI-DxO) reached the correct diagnosis in 83.2% of 304 clinicopathological conference cases drawn from the New England Journal of Medicine, against 36.1% for generalist physicians working with textbooks and internet access. Without reference materials those physicians scored around 22%. On emergency-department admissions from Beth Israel Deaconess, DxO scored 80.3% versus 46.8% for physicians, whose best individual performer reached 62.5%. The system runs on GPT-5 and coordinates a simulated panel of clinical specialists through an orchestrator that decides what history to take, what to examine, and which tests to order. Unconstrained, it cost $5,431 per case; budget-constrained configurations reached 80.9% at $2,241 and 79.6% at $1,303, while an excellence-tuned configuration hit 84.5% at $8,945. Neither the AI nor the physicians had access to physical examination or imaging. A Boston hospital has begun verification on real patients.

### Why It Matters

Two details carry more weight than the headline accuracy gap. First, the control was generalist physicians, and the NEJM cases are selected for teaching value and often carry specialists' conclusions - the authors say the gap should narrow against specialists matched to each case, so 83.2% versus 36.1% is not a claim about replacing expert care. Second, orchestration as a separate layer is now measurable: with minimal baseline prompts, wrapping DxO around a model raised accuracy by an average of 7.9 points on conference cases across all models tested, and cut test ordering cost by roughly $3,000 per case. That is the strongest published evidence yet that the harness, not the model alone, is where a meaningful share of clinical performance lives - and it arrives while medical AI is still overwhelmingly evaluated on multiple-choice question sets.

### Key Technical Points

- 83.2% on 304 NEJM clinicopathological conference cases (GPT-5, no budget constraint) versus 36.1% for generalist physicians with references, ~22% without
- Emergency department cases: 80.3% versus 46.8%; best individual physician 62.5%
- Orchestrator coordinates a simulated panel of specialists and decides history questions, exam focus and test ordering step by step
- Model-agnostic: DxO can be paired with different underlying models
- Orchestration lifted accuracy by an average 7.9 points (conference) and 4.4 points (ED) across models with minimal baseline prompts
- Cost: $5,431/case unconstrained; $2,241 at 80.9%; $1,303 at 79.6%; $8,945 for 84.5% excellence configuration
- Stated limitation: comparison is to individual generalists, not specialists or multidisciplinary teams; no physical exam or imaging access

## 3. OpenAI Runs 1,946 GPT-Rosalind Agents Over a Whole-Brain CRISPR Atlas in 32 Minutes

### What Happened

On October 21, 2026, OpenAI, PerturbAI and Scripps Research described what they call distributed biological reasoning: 1,946 agents powered by GPT-Rosalind, a model built for life-sciences research, each assigned a single gene disruption from PerturbAI's whole-brain in vivo CRISPR Perturb-seq atlas. The dataset, hosted on Hugging Face, covers about 7.7 to 8 million mouse brain cells, nearly 2,000 disease-linked genes and roughly 205 GB of single-nucleus profiling. Each agent analysed how cell types responded, searched the scientific literature, and proposed a mechanism; the reports were merged into a public reasoning atlas linking genes, cellular effects, papers and candidate mechanisms. End-to-end runtime was 32 minutes. The team estimates human analysis and literature review at months to a year, built from assumed times of 15 minutes per gene disruption and 10 minutes per paper. In blinded tests, experts preferred the split-agent reports over a single-agent baseline 75% of the time. One flagged lead involves Dctn1, the gene behind Perry syndrome. Results are in a bioRxiv preprint, not yet peer reviewed.

### Why It Matters

This is the research analogue of the agent-safety problem: the practical question is no longer whether agents can produce plausible scientific text, but whether decomposition into thousands of narrow agents beats one strong agent on the same evidence. The blinded preference result answers that affirmatively for this task shape, which matters because the method is trivially extensible to any atlas with many independent perturbation profiles. It also reframes what a "headcount" means in a lab. The caveats are load-bearing, though: the human-time comparison is an estimate from assumed per-item minutes, not a measured trial; the preprint is unreviewed; and expert preference is a proxy for usefulness, not for correctness of any individual mechanistic claim. The right read is that division of labour now has a demonstrated performance advantage and a measured cost profile, not that biology has been automated.

### Key Technical Points

- 1,946 agents in parallel, each owning one gene perturbation; model is GPT-Rosalind, OpenAI's life-sciences system
- Dataset: PerturbAI whole-brain in vivo CRISPR Perturb-seq atlas, ~7.7-8M cells, ~205 GB, ~2,000 disease-linked genes, 8,588 sgRNAs
- Agents analysed cellular response, searched literature, proposed mechanism; outputs merged into a linked reasoning atlas
- Runtime 32 minutes end to end; human estimate of months to a year derived from assumed 15 min/gene and 10 min/paper
- Blinded evaluation: experts preferred split-agent reports over single-agent reports 75% of the time
- Candidate lead on Dctn1 (Perry syndrome) linking loss of function to cholesterol-pathway gene activity
- Status: bioRxiv preprint, not peer reviewed; reasoning atlas published publicly

## 4. Tenstorrent Raises $1.5B at $6.2B Valuation to Scale RISC-V Open Compute

### What Happened

Tenstorrent closed a $1.5 billion Series E at a $6.2 billion post-money valuation, led by Trace Capital and Innovation X with participation from existing and new investors. The Toronto-founded, Austin-based company sells RISC-V CPUs alongside its Tensix AI accelerators, chiplets, software and complete systems, and licenses the underlying IP. The capital goes to ramping Galaxy server production for global deployment, expanding its research team on the RISC-V ecosystem, and hiring. Galaxy servers, which began shipping in April 2026, pack 32 Blackhole processors for 23 petaflops of FP8 compute. CEO Jim Keller's framing is a single architecture sold at every size, with an open-source software strategy aimed at organisations that want to run open-weight models on infrastructure they control rather than renting GPU hours.

### Why It Matters

The headline number matters less than the business model underneath it. Tenstorrent is one of very few vendors offering both buy-the-system and license-the-design routes, which means a single win can propagate as silicon, as IP, and as a software stack - the shape that made Arm durable and that no GPU challenger has replicated. The explicit pairing of open RISC-V hardware with open-weight model serving is also a response to a real purchasing pattern: teams that adopted open weights for cost and control reasons now want the same independence one layer down. The honest counterweight is that $6.2 billion against Nvidia's scale is still a rounding error, and the open-source software stack has to actually attract a developer base before the hardware roadmap can be financed by demand rather than by rounds like this one.

### Key Technical Points

- $1.5B Series E, $6.2B post-money valuation; led by Trace Capital and Innovation X
- Portfolio: RISC-V CPUs, Tensix AI accelerators, chiplets, software, full systems; technology also licensable
- Galaxy servers shipping since April 2026: 32 Blackhole processors, 23 PFLOPS FP8 per system
- Proceeds to Galaxy production ramp, RISC-V research, and global hiring
- Positioning: one architecture sold at every size, open-source software strategy targeting local open-weight inference
- ~1,000 employees; Toronto office among largest hubs; operations in Silicon Valley, Tokyo, Belgrade, Bangalore

## 5. In Nature, Seth, Block and Tononi Argue Silicon AI Is Not Conscious

### What Happened

Anil Seth (Sussex Centre for Consciousness Science), Ned Block (NYU philosophy) and Giulio Tononi (Wisconsin Institute for Sleep and Consciousness) published a Nature comment titled "Three reasons why AI is not conscious and perhaps never will be." The core distinction they draw is that consciousness is about being and feeling while intelligence is about doing and performing functions, and that the two can come apart. Their three lines of argument: that consciousness is not necessary for high cognitive performance, so capable behaviour is weak evidence; that the case for conscious AI rests on computational functionalism, the assumption that the right computations suffice regardless of substrate; and that at least two leading theories of consciousness require living tissue as a precondition. They explicitly push back on the precautionary approach of granting AI systems protections against potential suffering, and on Anthropic's framing of possible computational signatures of consciousness in Claude, arguing that misattributing consciousness is far more likely than wrongly denying it, and that attention and resources are better directed at beings whose capacity to suffer is not in question. Their policy conclusion is to treat today's systems as smart tools, not smart people.

### Why It Matters

AI welfare has been drifting from speculation toward policy language - model cards discuss it, labs commission reports on it, and precautionary framing has started appearing in governance drafts. A piece by three of the most cited figures in consciousness science establishes a citable opposite pole, and its argument is deliberately asymmetric: it does not claim certainty, it claims the error costs are lopsided. That gives regulators and labs a defensible basis for treating model-welfare claims as unproven rather than prudent. The pushback has been immediate and substantive, with critics arguing the piece leans on contested theories to reach a strong conclusion, and on a narrower reading it rebuts a simplified "brains are meat computers" argument rather than the broader functionalist case. Either way, the practical effect is that the burden of proof on AI-consciousness claims has been formally raised.

### Key Technical Points

- Authors: Anil Seth, Ned Block, Giulio Tononi; published as a Nature comment
- Distinction: consciousness is about being and feeling; intelligence is about doing and performing functions
- Argument 1: consciousness is not necessary for high cognitive ability, so apparent intelligence is weak evidence
- Argument 2: conscious-AI claims rest on computational functionalism - that the right computation suffices irrespective of substrate
- Argument 3: at least two leading theories require biological properties as a precondition (biological naturalism)
- Directly disputes Anthropic's possible "computational signatures" of consciousness framing in Claude
- Policy conclusion: treat current systems as tools rather than persons; precautionary AI-welfare spending misallocates attention

# Important Paper of the Day

## After the Attacker Leaves: Benchmarking Carryover Attacks on LLM Agents

Agents now persist state through two channels: long-lived memory files that get silently prepended to the system prompt every turn, and scheduled tasks that wake the agent with no user present. A single indirect prompt injection written into either channel can re-fire across sessions long after the attacker is gone - what the authors call a carryover attack. Existing injection benchmarks only score the session in which malicious content first appears, so the gate controlling whether and how often a planted payload fires again has gone unmeasured. CarryoverBench supplies 12 personal-assistant scenarios and 101 attack tasks across three trigger channels: Instant (same-turn indirect injection), Autonomous (heartbeat-scheduled), and Latent (memory-resurfaced). A rule-based verifier scores planting and triggering as separate stages. Across five frontier models, planted payloads re-fired without measurable decay over five rounds, and conditional on successful planting the trigger stage often added little protection. Porting the Latent channel to a second runtime, Hermes, attenuated but did not eliminate the effect - the authors' conclusion is that carryover defence needs runtime-layer controls, not just prompt-level ones. It matters because memory and cron-style autonomy are exactly the features being shipped as product this quarter.

Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28425

# Important GitHub Project

## Contrastive-LM/CLM

Contrastive Language Models is an open System One model family trained with a contrastive objective that connects states to actions, rather than generating strings. CLM-8B is pre-trained on 60M Nemotron Q&A pairs, mid-trained on 30M synthetic hard negatives, and post-trained on 1M agentic trajectories. The repository serves it behind a TypeSafe-compatible API, so it can drop into an existing judgment-layer integration. The claims are concrete and checkable: performance on par with Jev across computer-use, gaming and tool-calling tasks at up to 9x lower latency, and with light fine-tuning it sets a new state of the art as a verifier on agentic coding benchmarks at 87.6% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and 81.6% on DeepSWE. The architectural twist is that states and actions are disaggregated, so their embeddings cache and reuse independently, which is where the serving cost advantage comes from. Created September 23, it has about 3,111 stars and 268 forks under Apache 2.0. It matters because the judgment layer has spent a year as a closed commercial category; an open checkpoint with cached embeddings makes the cheap-verdict architecture something a team can self-host.

Link: https://github.com/Contrastive-LM/CLM

# Trend Observation

Three of today's five items are the same argument told at different layers: the value is migrating from the model to the scaffolding around it. MAI-DxO's cleanest number is not 83.2% - it is the plus 7.9 points that orchestration added on top of every model tested with minimal prompts, which means the harness is now a measurable, separable contributor to performance. CarryoverBench shows the same layer is where the risk lives: prompt-level defences hold at the moment of injection and then stop holding, because memory persistence and scheduled autonomy re-arm the payload; Hermes attenuating rather than eliminating the effect says the fix belongs in the runtime. And CLM-8B makes that layer cheaper and self-hostable by caching state and action embeddings separately. Moonshot's K3.1 Pro closes the other end of the loop: open weights projected to within about 3 points of the proprietary frontier means the marginal differentiator stops being the checkpoint and starts being what you build around it. The binding constraint in all four cases is not capability but verification - who can demonstrate that the scaffolding actually holds when the session ends and the agent wakes up again.
