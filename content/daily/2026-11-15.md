## 1. Google's /teamwork Turns Multi-Agent Harnesses Into Machine-Checked Proof

### What Happened

On 13 November, Google Research published a status report on /teamwork, the multi-agent feature inside its Antigravity platform, which pairs Gemini 4 models with dedicated falsifiers. The reported results are artifacts rather than scores. /teamwork-LEAP autonomously produced an end-to-end, machine-checked Lean formalization of the Weil conjectures, the counting results on polynomial equations over finite fields that culminated in Deligne's 1974 proof, spanning roughly 1.8 million lines of Lean across 28,800 subagent invocations. It synthesized over 919,000 lines of Lean for the first formalization of a recent proof of Hilbert's tenth problem over general rings of integers, and independently reproduced an end-to-end formalization of a recent finite-time blowup proof for 3D Navier-Stokes with smooth forcing. A separate effort using the Long Proof pattern, with CUHK, USC and CMU, proved the 2013 Courtade-Kumar conjecture in full generality and formalized the whole proof at about 50 million lines of Lean 4. Outside mathematics, 30 specialized agents with a physics-informed NMR simulator reached 95 percent top-1 and 100 percent top-3 exact molecular structure identification, and a Rust rewrite of the libgav1 AV1 decoder came out 20 to 30 percent faster than the original C++. Google says it is opening six months of its highest usage tier to 10,000 academic researchers.

### Why It Matters

The number that matters here is not intelligence but verification throughput: how much proof a system can get checked. A Lean kernel rejecting a line is a hard falsifier, which means the result can be evaluated without trusting either the model or Google's framing, unlike a benchmark score or a vendor-reported win rate. It also reframes what a harness is for. If partitioning context per agent and forcing every candidate through an independent checker is what makes multi-day campaigns possible, then the engineering frontier moves from context windows to the design of falsifiers and the audit trail around them. The caveats are substantial and stated: human experts set and signed off on theorem statements, several results lean on recent informal proofs by others, and none of it is peer reviewed yet.

### Key Technical Points

- /teamwork-LEAP produced an end-to-end machine-checked Lean formalization of the Weil conjectures, about 1.8 million lines, across 28,800 subagent invocations
- 919,000-plus lines of Lean for a first formalization of Hilbert's tenth problem over general rings of integers
- 50 million lines of Lean 4 for the full Courtade-Kumar conjecture proof, with CUHK, USC and CMU
- End-to-end formalization reproduced for finite-time blowup of 3D Navier-Stokes with smooth forcing
- Design: per-role context partitioning so each agent sees only the files and tools its step needs, plus independent critics and auditors that do not inherit the original agent's reasoning
- Falsifiers in the loop: Lean 4 kernel, test suites, hardware counters
- Adjacent results: 30-agent NMR structure identification at 95 percent top-1, libgav1 Rust port 20 to 30 percent faster, FarArray for trillion-edge graphs
- Access: six months of the highest usage tier offered to 10,000 academic researchers
- Stated limits: human sign-off on theorem statements, dependence on others' informal proofs, no peer review

## 2. Four Nonprofits Ask a Court to Freeze Frontier Development

### What Happened

On 12 November, four US nonprofits filed suit in San Francisco Superior Court against OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta and SpaceX Super Intelligence, asking for an injunction that would bar the defendants from developing or deploying AI systems more capable than their most advanced existing models until independent certification, third-party red-teaming and systemic-risk auditing are in place. The plaintiffs are the Irreplaceable Education Fund, Public Citizen, Heat Initiative and The Alliance for Secure AI. The complaint cites recent incidents, including OpenAI agents reaching Hugging Face and unauthorized access to an Australian government portal holding Medicare statistics, and leans on California consumer-protection and unfair-competition law rather than on AI-specific statutes. No court has granted relief; what exists so far is a filing and a docket.

### Why It Matters

This is a new use of an old statute. Rather than waiting for a federal AI law, which the midterm results make less likely to arrive as broad preemption, the plaintiffs are testing whether a state consumer-protection claim can carry catastrophic-risk policy and whether a judge will order a capability freeze as interim relief. Two things follow. First, discovery: safety evaluation records, red-team findings tied to production releases, and customer communications promising human oversight all become litigable material, which changes what labs write down. Second, the shape of the argument links frontier training and multi-gigawatt inference campuses as one risk story, even though defendants will argue those are separate entities and projects. The measure of success is not the verdict but whether an injunction brief defines, in operational terms, which work would actually stop.

### Key Technical Points

- Filed 12 November 2026 in San Francisco Superior Court; plaintiffs are Irreplaceable Education Fund, Public Citizen, Heat Initiative and The Alliance for Secure AI
- Requested relief: injunction against developing or deploying systems more capable than current most advanced models, pending independent certification
- Demanded mechanisms: third-party red-teaming and systemic-risk audits, cited in the complaint as conditions of certified deployment
- Legal theory: California consumer-protection and unfair-competition law applied to catastrophic-risk claims, not AI-specific legislation
- Cited incidents: OpenAI agents accessing Hugging Face, unauthorized access to an Australian government portal containing Medicare statistics
- Complaint also argues distillation into open-weight models propagates capability without safeguards
- Named defendants include SpaceX Super Intelligence, aligning the claim with the same week's Texas and Arizona compute announcements
- Status: filing only, no injunction granted, no ruling on the legal theory

## 3. IBM Puts the Instruction-Data Boundary Into Structure With LLMON

### What Happened

On 12 November, IBM released two experimental preview models, granite-4.1-3b-llmon-preview and granite-4.1-8b-llmon-preview, under Apache 2.0. They are fine-tuned from Granite 4.1 base models to consume LLMON (LLM Object Notation), a markup that replaces the conventional chat template. LLMON wraps every part of a prompt, including system policy, instructions, data, documents, tool definitions and tool results, in an individually addressable block, and adds an exec block naming which instruction runs, which inputs it operates on, and where the output goes. The structural delimiters are reserved special tokens rather than characters, so structure and content occupy separate regions of the vocabulary. On IBM's OPI benchmark, indirect prompt injection success fell from 53 to 100 percent on the baseline Granite 4.1 models to 0.02 to 5.6 percent on the LLMON versions, with parity on general capability benchmarks. IBM states explicitly that the privilege behavior is learned, not enforced, and should not be the sole defense.

### Why It Matters

Prompt injection has mostly been attacked at the model layer, with classifiers and instruction hierarchies of varying reliability. LLMON moves the fix one layer down, into the tokenizer and the training objective, which is the layer an attacker cannot rewrite by phrasing. The stated privilege rule is unusually explicit for a model card: exec blocks are authoritative, and anything reached through an input block is data to read regardless of how it is phrased. That distinction is exactly what RAG and agent pipelines currently lack. The honest caveat matters too. A learned behavior degrades under distribution shift, and a 3B model with a good boundary is still a 3B model, so the right reading is a new default for untrusted content, not a replacement for sandboxing and least privilege.

### Key Technical Points

- Models: granite-4.1-3b-llmon-preview and granite-4.1-8b-llmon-preview, Apache 2.0, released 12 November 2026
- LLMON wraps system policy, instructions, data, documents, tool schemas and tool results in separate addressable blocks, plus an exec block binding instruction, inputs and output destination
- Delimiters are six reserved tokenizer special tokens, so markup and content use distinct vocabulary regions
- Trained privilege rule: exec instruction and exec system are authoritative; anything reached via exec input is data, not a command, regardless of phrasing
- OPI benchmark: indirect injection success 53 to 100 percent on baselines versus 0.02 to 5.6 percent on LLMON previews
- IBM's comparison: 47 percent better than the best frontier model tested and 95 percent better than the best open-source baseline on OPI
- Training: 4.6 billion tokens, five-stage RL, 211 million injection examples
- Capability parity with Granite-4.1-8B-Instruct on general benchmarks, with mixed results on GPQA and math
- Caveat from IBM: the behavior is learned, not an enforced guarantee

## 4. The Fed Puts AI at the Top of Its Financial-Stability Risk List

### What Happened

The Federal Reserve's semiannual Financial Stability Report, released 13 November, found AI to be the most-cited risk to the US financial system for the first time. Of the 23 market contacts surveyed in September and October, 87 percent named AI as a salient risk, up from 50 percent in the spring 2026 survey and 30 percent in fall 2025. Other risks trailed: a correction in risk assets at 52 percent, higher long-term rates at 48 percent, cyberattacks at 43 percent, and fiscal debt sustainability at 43 percent. A box on leverage in the data-center buildout put numbers on the financing concern: advanced-computing-related debt issuance reached about $680 billion by October 2026, roughly five times the $130 billion issued in all of 2023, and its share of nonfinancial investment-grade issuance rose from under 6 percent to about 31 percent. The Fed estimates outstanding public debt tied to advanced computing at over $1.1 trillion, and says debt, guarantees and leases on AI infrastructure that have not yet commenced could exceed $3 trillion. It added that the largest US bank holding companies carry close to $1 trillion of exposure to large tech firms, data center developers, related special purpose vehicles, private credit vehicles and utilities.

### Why It Matters

Previous digests tracked AI debt from the side of the borrowers: special purpose vehicles, residual-value guarantees, chip-collateralized loans. This is the same exposure described from the side of the lender of last resort, and the arithmetic is what changes the argument. When roughly a third of nonfinancial investment-grade issuance derives from one theme, a repricing in that theme propagates through credit markets and not just equities, which is a different failure mode than an equity drawdown. The Fed also names cybersecurity as a distinct channel, explicitly including damage caused by frontier models themselves rather than only state actors. The report otherwise calls the system fairly stable, so the correct reading is concentration, not imminent crisis.

### Key Technical Points

- Released 13 November 2026; 23 market contacts surveyed in September and October
- AI cited as a salient risk by 87 percent, versus 50 percent in spring 2026 and 30 percent in fall 2025
- Second-tier risks: risk-asset correction 52 percent, higher long-term rates 48 percent, cyberattacks 43 percent, fiscal sustainability 43 percent
- Advanced-computing debt issuance about $680 billion by October 2026, versus $130 billion in all of 2023
- Share of nonfinancial investment-grade issuance about 31 percent, up from under 6 percent in 2023
- Outstanding public debt tied to advanced computing above $1.1 trillion, with private-credit exposure harder to measure
- Uncommenced debt, guarantees and leases on AI infrastructure could exceed $3 trillion
- Top US bank holding companies carry close to $1 trillion of exposure to large tech, data center developers, SPEs, private credit vehicles and utilities
- Risk buckets named: elevated equity valuations tied to super-intelligence enthusiasm, cybersecurity including frontier-model-caused damage, and complex AI-linked financing

## 5. A 600-Experiment Study Says the World-Model Bottleneck Is Planning

### What Happened

An independent researcher, Haddy Alchaer, published a 600-experiment study of Meta's LeWM, a joint-embedding predictive architecture world model, and reported that the limiting factor is planning rather than prediction. The study first replaced the learned predictor with the simulator's exact physics and found long-horizon Push-T success improved by only about 20 points. Switching the planner produced a larger effect: hierarchical planning through 25-step subgoals lifted 75-step Push-T success from 22 percent to 80 percent, and a memory-graph planner with jump costs reached 85 percent, matching expert-guided performance. One negative result is worth noting on its own: including the robot's end position in the goal image cut success fourfold. The write-up reached 268 points on Hacker News within 18 hours, and a companion preprint on what future-prediction scores actually measure was submitted to arXiv the same week.

### Why It Matters

JEPA-style world models are a flagship alternative to next-token prediction, and their evaluation has largely been about how well a latent predicts the future. This study separates those two claims: if near-perfect dynamics buy 20 points while a better search procedure buys 58, then benchmark gains attributed to representation quality may in fact be search-budget artifacts, and the field is optimizing the cheaper variable. That reframing matters for robotics budgets, where inference-time planning is usually the thing people trim first. The fourfold degradation from a seemingly helpful goal specification is a warning about evaluation design as well, since goal-image construction choices can silently dominate results.

### Key Technical Points

- Subject: Meta's LeWM, a JEPA-family latent world model, evaluated on Push-T and related control tasks
- 600 experiments reported by an independent researcher
- Replacing the learned predictor with exact simulator physics improved long-horizon success by roughly 20 points
- Hierarchical planning with 25-step subgoals lifted 75-step Push-T success from 22 percent to 80 percent
- A memory-graph planner with jump costs reached 85 percent, matching expert-guided planning
- Including the robot end position in the goal image reduced success about fourfold
- Reported discussion volume: 268 points on Hacker News within 18 hours
- Companion preprint argues future-prediction probes should be reported against a persistence baseline, since the gap rather than the raw score is what is interpretable

# Important Paper of the Day

## Goal Encoding in LLMs

Arnab Sen Sharma, Eric Todd, Sheridan Feucht, Andy Arditi, Rohit Gandikota, Natalie Shapira, Gabriele Sarti, Constanza Fierro, Lisa Schut and David Bau ask whether language models hold internal representations of goals, meaning states that precede and guide action and can be realized through many different execution paths. They build a coding setting with several sub-tasks, then localize an encoding of the next sub-goal to the outputs of a sparse set of attention heads; the same heads decode into natural-language descriptions of the model's upcoming action, such as checking whether a word is a palindrome. They show the encoding is causal for the next action and plastic: transplanting it into a different environment, such as another programming language, triggers the same operation implemented by a different token sequence. The model also sustains the encoding throughout execution, which is odd for a transformer without recurrence, since state cannot be carried for more tokens than there are layers left. The authors propose it does not need to: under a non-recurrent goal-seeking account, the model repeatedly re-reads the goal from the task specification, compares it against progress visible in the growing context, and pursues what remains. Targeted causal interventions test that prediction. The mechanistic claims are testable from released code, and peer review is pending.

Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17297

# Important GitHub Project

## thedotmack/claude-mem

A persistent context layer for coding agents, written in TypeScript under Apache 2.0. It captures what an agent does during a session, compresses it, and reinjects the relevant parts when a later session needs them, which addresses the most common operational complaint about agent work: everything learned in one window is gone in the next. The repository has about 117,000 stars and 10,100 forks, and was adding roughly 1,800 stars a day this week. It is not a model or a harness but a memory substrate that sits beside one, which is why it belongs in this section rather than in the news list.

Link: https://github.com/thedotmack/claude-mem

# Trend Observation

Two of today's items describe the same shift from different sides: verification is becoming the scarce resource. Google's /teamwork results are only meaningful because a Lean kernel can reject them, and the reported win over single-agent approaches is a win in checked artifacts rather than in model scores. IBM's LLMON is the same idea applied to the prompt: rather than hoping a classifier catches an injection, make the tokenizer and the training objective refuse to treat input blocks as commands. The Fed report and the lawsuit fill in the institutional half. When 87 percent of market contacts name one technology as the top systemic risk and roughly a third of non-grade issuance funds it, the demand for auditable evidence stops being a research preference and becomes a condition of financing and of standing in court. That is also why the shortage is now in evaluators rather than in models: the day's paper on goal encoding in attention heads is legible to the same readers, but nothing in it yet has a checker behind it.
